Where AI-safety money goes, beside where the experts look

MIT's AI Risk Repository gives AI risk 24 coordinates and an expert survey over them. We put five funders' public grant records into the same coordinates. The two pictures do not match, and the table below says exactly where. Nothing here is a ranking of what to fund; it is a map of attention — expert and financial — with the noise on both sides shown.

What the table says

The experts' top three have almost no money. Dangerous capabilities 7.2 is the exception at 8%. Weapons & cyberattacks 4.2 (rank 2 by expected severity) has $2.3M — 0.3% — and that is not a scope artefact: Coefficient's biosecurity and GCR funds were read grant by grant, and the AI-related grants there are field-building, not AI-enabled bio or cyber defence. Power centralization 6.1 (rank 3 by severity, rank 2 by expert concern) has $5.4M — 0.7% — most of it one FLI request for proposals.
Six labels hold 91% of the money. Field-building and general support 43%, governance 23%, misalignment 9%, capability evaluations 8%, interpretability 4%, AI-system security 4%. The portfolio is concentrated in a way the expert signal is not.
A risk taxonomy is not a method taxonomy — and the table can mislead where they cross. Transparency & interpretability 7.4 is last of 24 as a risk in the expert survey and fifth by money. A second labelling by method resolves it: its $34M is $30M of interpretability research, funded because it serves misalignment and evaluations, not because anyone rates opacity as a top harm. Read 7.4 as a method. 6.1 and 4.2 stay empty under every method — that is the difference between "filed elsewhere" and "not funded".
The expert side is noisy, and the noise is shown. Resampling experts within each subdomain keeps 83% of pairwise orderings; the first seven subdomains sit within five percentage points of each other with standard errors of 1–2.5 points. The rank intervals in the chart are wide on purpose. Reading any single rank difference as a fact would be a mistake the data does not support.

The same dollars, by what kind of work they buy

Every grant also carries a method label: one of the 23 mitigation controls in MIT's draft control taxonomy, or — because that taxonomy has no place for research, talent or advocacy, which is most of what grants pay for — one of seven labels of ours, marked X.*.

Bars in blue are our labels; bars in ochre are MIT control subcategories (1.2 risk management & regulation, 3.1 testing & evaluation, 2.1 model security, 4.5 third-party access, 2.3 safety engineering).

How to read it, and how not to

  • Not cost-effectiveness. Nobody has measured what a dollar buys in any subdomain; the MIT mitigation database carries no effectiveness or cost. A low-money, high-severity row is a reason to ask why, not a conclusion.
  • Five funders, public records only. Corporate labs, governments, most academic funding and private donors are invisible. Coefficient Giving alone is $305M of the $338M in field; its grants carry a title and an organisation, no description.
  • Labels are model-produced against a written rubric, with a second independent labelling of 120 control grants: 91% exact agreement on the subdomain, 93% on the MIT domain. "General support of X" is labelled by what X mainly does — a judgement about the organisation, the largest source of error. A human-labelled control sample is the next step.
  • Amounts differ in kind. Coefficient and EA Funds publish granted amounts; SFF and FLI recommended amounts; Manifund the money actually raised. Years are award years.
  • The expert number is a mean subjective probability on a five-level harm scale, from 23–116 experts per subdomain; it is not a magnitude, and the "pragmatic mitigations" scenario it was paired with is a single unspecified hypothetical.
